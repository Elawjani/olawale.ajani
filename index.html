1 · Research synthesis

Turn scattered evidence into a design problem.

I start by clarifying the user goal, business objective, constraints and success criteria, then synthesise qualitative and quantitative signals into themes.

  • Separate observed behaviour from stakeholder assumptions.
  • Group recurring pain points and prioritise by user impact and business relevance.
  • Write the problem statement before committing to a UI pattern.

2 · Flow design

Make the journey understandable before making it polished.

Once the problem is framed, I map the primary journey and critical states. This helps expose unnecessary steps, missing information and edge cases early.

  • Map the happy path and high-risk exceptions.
  • Reduce decisions per screen and keep system feedback visible.
  • Align the information sequence with the user's mental model.

3 · Interface concept

Use hierarchy and progressive disclosure to lower cognitive load.

The polished concept translates the flow into a clear interface with predictable structure, strong hierarchy and accessible interaction patterns.

  • Prioritise one dominant action per state.
  • Use plain language and visible progress.
  • Keep validation close to the point of action.
Research before polish

Visual detail comes after the team agrees on the problem, user need and flow.

Accessibility by default

Hierarchy, language, states and contrast are considered as design inputs, not final QA.

Evaluate, then iterate

Usability findings and stakeholder feedback are treated as evidence for the next design decision.

Case study 02 · Financial services

Using customer insight to improve banking journeys

Context: At Union Bank, customer conversations and service data gave me direct insight into where retail and commercial banking journeys were confusing, repetitive or difficult to understand.

Signal

Support questions are product data.

Repeated questions, hesitation points and requests for help often reveal where a financial product or service is not self-explanatory.

  • Capture recurring questions and moments of uncertainty.
  • Compare qualitative feedback with service/performance information.
  • Separate policy constraints from avoidable UX friction.

Domain case study 03 · Public service & community

Designing for complex, high-stakes service journeys

Context: My government and nonprofit work involves people navigating multiple agencies, eligibility rules, documentation, safeguarding requirements and handoffs. This is a service-design lens drawn from those environments.

Service blueprint

Treat organisational handoffs as part of the user's experience.

In complex services, the experience is shaped not only by screens but also by agencies, policies, evidence requirements and handoffs. Mapping them together helps identify where users carry unnecessary administrative burden.

  • Map the user's goal alongside organisational responsibilities.
  • Surface documentation and eligibility requirements at the right time.
  • Design for stress, limited context and accessibility needs.
  • Make ownership and next steps explicit across handoffs.
